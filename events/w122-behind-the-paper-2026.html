Behind the Paper: How PPP research gets written, reviewed and published

CIB W122 webinar · 19 November 2026 · 4 min read, 70 min recording

Published papers rarely show how they came to be. Readers see the final argument, not the false starts, the reviewer comments, the reframing and the decisions behind it. Those are often the most useful lessons for doctoral candidates and early career researchers, and they are seldom discussed in the open. I organised and moderated this webinar for CIB W122, the International Council for Research and Innovation in Building and Construction’s working commission on public-private partnerships, where I have been communications coordinator since 2020. Two PPP researchers each took one of their own papers and walked through the path it took from first idea to publication.

Full recording, shared with the speakers’ agreement. Watch on YouTube.

The two papers

The papers were chosen to show contrasting journeys: one developed from a doctoral thesis and now widely cited, and one whose framing changed substantially during review. Registrants received full-text links when they signed up, so they could read both before the session.

  • Dr Carter B. Casady, Senior Research Engineer at Stanford University and Senior Fellow at George Mason University. Casady, C. B., Eriksson, K., Levitt, R. E., & Scott, W. R. (2020). (Re)defining public-private partnerships (PPPs) in the new public governance (NPG) paradigm: An institutional maturity perspective. Public Management Review, 22(2), 161–183. https://doi.org/10.1080/14719037.2019.1577909
  • Dr Gabriel Castelblanco, Assistant Professor at the University of Florida. Castelblanco, G., & Guevara, J. (2025). The theory and practice of unsolicited proposals for PPPs: Hidden fallacies, undervalued gains, and unintended consequences. Journal of Management in Engineering, 41(5), 04025044. https://doi.org/10.1061/JMENEA.MEENG-6615

How the session ran

Each speaker had about twenty minutes: roughly five on the paper and its findings, and fifteen on the story behind it, covering how the idea developed, how the writing went, what happened in review and what they would do differently now. A joint discussion followed, with questions collected from the Zoom chat and put to both speakers. There were enough of them that the session ran about ten minutes over.

The question that drew the most attention was how researchers in emerging PPP markets, where data and experienced experts are both scarce, can turn a context-specific study into a contribution to the international literature and meet the expectations of leading journals. Given who registered, that was not a side issue.

Who came

Sixty-five people registered from 21 countries across six continents. Attendance held at around forty throughout and peaked at 43, including the speakers and the W122 coordinators. Most registrants were from universities and research institutions, with others from industry and government.

By region, 32 registrants were in Asia (including the Middle East and Turkey), 9 in Europe, 8 in Oceania, 7 in Africa, 6 in North America and 3 in South America. By country: China 12, Australia 8, India 7, the United Kingdom 6, the United States 6, Indonesia 4, Pakistan 4, Nigeria 3, Malaysia 2, the Netherlands 2, and one each from Brazil, Chile, Colombia, Ethiopia, France, Kazakhstan, Kenya, Liberia, Oman, Turkey and Uganda.

Forty-one of the 65 were based in emerging and developing economies in Asia, Africa and Latin America. That is where PPP is widely used or being considered for infrastructure delivery now, and it is where the research interest appears to be.

The start time, 8 am in Beijing and 11 am in Sydney, suited the expected audience in Asia and Australia and both US-based speakers. It fell around midnight in Europe and Africa, which is the main reason the session was recorded.

What people said

Comments in the chat and messages afterwards said the same thing in different ways: participants valued how frank the speakers were about their publishing journeys, and doctoral candidates and early career researchers found the accounts relatable and encouraging. In his closing remarks in the chat, Professor Mohan Kumaraswamy thanked both speakers for ‘readily stepping forward to accept and deliver well on [the] invitation to share their frank experiences in a paper development-publication journey’.

What this record does not show

There was no formal survey, so the feedback above comes from the people who chose to write something, and they are the ones most likely to be pleased. The countries of those who joined live were not recorded either, so I cannot say how many of the registrants in Europe and Africa stayed up for it rather than waiting for the recording. Forty people in a room for seventy minutes is a reasonable turnout for a specialist webinar. It is not evidence that anyone’s next paper will be better.
